## Anti-dive and anti-squat (side-view geometry)

**Keys:** `under_lower_arm_shims_ff`, `under_lower_arm_shims_fr`, `under_lower_arm_shims_rf`, `under_lower_arm_shims_rr` — the **split** between the forward and rearward inner pickups on one axle (FF−FR front, RF−RR rear) tilts the lower arm's **inner hinge line in side view**. That inclination is the anti-geometry control. The **averaged** stack height on an axle is a separate mechanism — **roll centre / support** on that end (see **support-lower-inner** and **roll-centre**).

**Anti-dive** (front axle) and **anti-squat** (rear axle) orient the suspension's **side-view swing arm** so that **longitudinal** tire forces — braking at the front, drive at the rear — react through the **links** instead of compressing the springs. Two consequences:

1. **Less pitch** — the chassis dives/squats less for the same braking or drive force.
2. **Faster load transfer** — like most stiffness-type changes, anti geometry **increases the speed of load transfer**: the link path reacts force effectively instantly, where the spring/damper path takes time. The car takes its attitude and loads the tires **quicker** under brakes and throttle. The **settled total** of load transferred is unchanged — that is set by **CoG height and wheelbase**.

## Mechanism — wheel path under compression

Tilting the inner hinge line changes the direction the wheel travels (relative to the chassis) as the suspension compresses. Sign mapping confirmed for this sheet convention:

- **Rear, anti-squat:** hinge inclined so the wheel **sweeps rearward** as it compresses. On throttle, the forward drive force at the contact patch then works **against** compression — the rear squats less. **Raising the forward pickup of the rear arm (RF−RR more positive) = more anti-squat.**
- **Front, anti-dive:** hinge inclined so the wheel **sweeps forward** as it compresses. Under braking, the rearward force at the contact patch works against compression — the nose dives less. **Raising the rearward pickup of the front arm (FF−FR more negative) = more anti-dive.**

## Handling tendencies

**More rear anti-squat:**

- **Less squat on throttle** — the rear ride height holds up, keeping the front loaded longer, which often reads as **more steering on power** / less exit push.
- Drive force is partly reacted through the links, which can add **initial forward-drive feel** on corner exit.
- The trade: the rear can become **busier over bumps under power** — the same geometry that resists squat also makes drive force fight bump compliance. On rough or low-grip surfaces this **sometimes** costs traction rather than adding it. **Test** rather than assume.

**More front anti-dive:**

- **Less nose dive on brakes** — a flatter platform into the corner, calmer pitch transient, and steering geometry (caster/camber) that changes less during braking.
- The trade: braking force fights bump compliance at the front, so the front can feel **harsher over bumps while braking** and turn-in over kerbs or broken surfaces can lose compliance.

**Less anti (either end)** gives the mirror image: more pitch movement, slower attitude change under longitudinal force, generally **more compliance over bumps** under braking/power at that end.

## Where it sits among the levers

Anti-geometries act through **longitudinal** force — strongest under braking (entry) and throttle (exit). In real racing the car is almost never in pure steady-state cornering — some brake or throttle is nearly always present — so the influence **blends through the whole corner** rather than switching off mid-corner; only the idealized zero-throttle steady state is (nearly) untouched. Complementary to springs and damper oil, which shape pitch **rate** at all times (see **spring-rate**, **damper-oil**).

Small splits are normal; large splits are an unusual, aggressive geometry — check the field position before recommending going further out.
