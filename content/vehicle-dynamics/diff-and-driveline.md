## Differential (rear gear diff)
**Keys:** `diff_oil`
**Concepts:** [[differential-coupling]]

**Physics.** Thicker diff oil raises the diff's **coupling** — it resists a speed difference between the
rear wheels more strongly; thinner frees it. (Ball-diff tightness, one-ways, and a spool are other ways
to set coupling on cars that use them.)

## Differential — rotation direction (on power vs off power)

**Sign (founder-confirmed 2026-07-31):** more coupling (thicker `diff_oil`) **restricts rotation off
throttle** and **increases rotation on throttle**. Thinner is the mirror.

**Off throttle** there is no drive torque to share, so the diff only resists the speed difference the
arc demands. More coupling pulls the rear wheels toward one speed, opposing the yaw the corner asks
for.

**On throttle** the [[friction-circle]] decides it. More coupling stops the lightly loaded inner rear
spinning up and shedding torque, so both rear tyres carry more longitudinal force — and grip spent
lengthways is not available sideways, so less of the rear's grip budget is left for cornering. A
freer diff lets the inner spin and leaves more of that budget available.

**What makes the change bigger or smaller.** Both effects grow with **how hard the rear wheels are
being forced to differ in speed** — the more the car is rotating for its speed, the more coupling
there is to fight, which is why a tight corner shows it more than a fast one. The on-throttle
effect also grows the **less grip there is to spend**, because the same drive demand then eats a
bigger share of the budget. Neither scales with how long the corner lasts, so this is a separate
question from which phase the driver is in ([[corner-regime]], [[bite-hold]]).

## Front driveline (spool) — context, not a lever
Touring cars run a **spool** front: both front wheels locked to one speed, no coupling knob. It
interacts with steering because steering asks the front wheels for *different* speeds while the spool
forbids it — see steering-geometry-ackermann.
